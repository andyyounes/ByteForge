1.1 Evolution & Classification of Computers
Three eras, five generations, and three different ways to classify a computer.
Key Terms
Analog computer
Processes continuously varying physical quantities (pressure, temperature, speed) — e.g. a car's speedometer.
Digital computer
Processes information using the binary number system (0s and 1s) — the most common type today.
General purpose computer
Can run many different programs for many different tasks — e.g. a desktop, laptop or smartphone.
Special purpose computer
Built to repeatedly do ONE job very efficiently — e.g. an ATM or a traffic light controller.
Theory — Three Eras of Computing

(i) Mechanical Era (Dark Age)

  • Manually-operated machines, invented before electricity — they helped with arithmetic but had no "processing" of their own.
  • Abacus (3000 B.C.) — the first computer prototype, still used today to teach basic arithmetic.
  • Napier's Bones (1612) — John Napier's rods, used for multiplication and division.
  • Slide Rule (1622) — William Oughtred's pair of moveable ruled scales, used to multiply and divide.
  • Pascaline (1642) — Blaise Pascal's mechanical calculator; could add and subtract using adjustable wheels.
  • Leibniz's Calculator (1694) — the first calculator that could do all four basic operations: add, subtract, multiply, divide.
  • Babbage's Engines (1822 / 1837) — Charles Babbage, the "Father of the Computer", built the Difference Engine (an automatic mechanical calculator with storage) and designed (but never finished) the Analytical Engine — considered the first general-purpose computer design, programmed using punched cards.

(ii) Electro-Mechanical Era (Middle Age)

  • Starting mid-19th century — machines powered by steam and electricity became faster and more accurate.
  • Hollerith's Tabulating Machine (1890) — Herman Hollerith's invention compiled the entire 1890 U.S. census in 6 months, versus 7 years for the 1880 census done by hand.

(iii) Electronic Era (Modern Age) — the Five Generations

True computers — working on input, store, process, output — split into 5 generations by their core technology:

1st (1940–56)
Vacuum tubes. Huge, used machine language, magnetic drums + punched cards. Examples: ENIAC, UNIVAC.
2nd (1956–63)
Transistors. Smaller, faster, cheaper. Introduced assembly language and FORTRAN. Examples: IBM 7094, IBM 1401.
3rd (1964–71)
Integrated Circuits (ICs). Keyboard + monitor became standard. High-level languages like Pascal, COBOL. Examples: IBM 360/370.
4th (1971–now)
Microprocessors. RAM/ROM, GUIs, languages like C/C++/Java. Examples: Apple Macintosh, IBM PC.
5th (now & beyond)
AI-based. Self-learning, reasoning machines — Machine Learning, Robotics, Natural Language Processing.
Theory — Classifying Computers (Three Ways)

By technology (type of data handled)

  • Analog — continuously varying physical quantities (speedometer, voltmeter).
  • Digital — binary data (0s and 1s); the most common type everywhere.
  • Hybrid — combines analog and digital in one machine, converting between the two.

By size

  • Super Computer — most powerful/fastest/largest; used for weather forecasting, nuclear research, aerodynamics.
  • Mainframe — powerful multi-user/multi-processor; used by banks and large organizations.
  • Minicomputer — smaller than mainframe, bigger than micro; often used as network/internet servers (e.g. DEC VAX).
  • Microcomputer — a Personal Computer (PC); the smallest, designed for individual use.

By purpose

  • General Purpose — can run many different programs (desktops, laptops, tablets, smartphones).
  • Special Purpose / Dedicated — built for one repeated job (traffic lights, navigational systems, ATMs).
Practice — Test Yourself

These questions only cover what this subsection taught — the eras/generations of computers and how they're classified.

Short answer 1
Why is Charles Babbage known as the "Father of the Computer"?
Reveal answer

He designed the first complete computing machine (the Difference Engine) and designed the Analytical Engine — considered the first general-purpose computer design.

Short answer 2
Which generation of computers introduced the microprocessor, and what effect did this have?
Reveal answer

The 4th generation (1971–present). It made computers faster, smaller, less expensive, and more reliable, and led to GUIs becoming common.

Fill in the blank 3
An analog computer processes ______ quantities, while a digital computer processes data using the ______ number system.
Reveal answer

continuously varying (physical); binary.

Spot the error 4
A student says a Mainframe is bigger and more powerful than a Super Computer. What's wrong?
Reveal answer

It's the other way round — Super Computers are the most powerful, fastest, and largest. Mainframes are powerful multi-user machines, but a step below Super Computers.

Applied / scenario 5
A bank needs one computer that many employees can use simultaneously to process huge numbers of transactions. Which size category fits best, and why?
Reveal answer

A Mainframe — it's a powerful multi-user, multi-processor computer designed exactly for this kind of high-speed, simultaneous, large-scale use in banks and big organizations.

Short answer 6
Give one real example each of a general purpose computer and a special purpose computer.
Reveal answer

General purpose: a laptop or smartphone. Special purpose: an ATM, or a traffic light controller.
