8.01 Definitions of Data Integrity, Privacy and Security
Three closely related words that mean three genuinely different things — and one is a prerequisite for the other two.
Key Terms
Data integrity
A requirement for data to be accurate and up to date.
Data privacy
A requirement for data to be available only to authorised users.
Data security
A requirement for data to be recoverable if lost or corrupted.
Data protection law
A law that relates to data privacy.
Theory

Data integrity — easy to define, hard to ensure

  • Only ACCURATE and UP-TO-DATE data has integrity.
  • Any person or organisation storing data needs it to have integrity — but achieving this reliably is genuinely difficult.

Data privacy — about control, not secrecy for its own sake

  • Data privacy is about keeping data PRIVATE rather than letting it enter the public domain — it applies to both individuals and organisations.
  • Assuming an individual isn't engaged in criminal/subversive activity, they should be in CONTROL of which data about themselves is public vs. private.
  • For an individual, privacy has little chance of being upheld WITHOUT a legal framework penalising breaches — these are DATA PROTECTION LAWS.
  • Such laws mainly cover PERSONAL data supplied to an organisation for a specific, AGREED purpose — obliging the organisation to protect its privacy and integrity.
  • Laws don't guarantee compliance, but they act as a DETERRENT because wrongdoers can face legal proceedings.

Data security — availability and unchanged-ness

  • Data is "secure" if it's AVAILABLE for use when needed, AND the data made available is exactly what was originally stored.
  • Security is BREACHED if data has been lost or corrupted.

The crucial relationship between the three

  • Data SECURITY is a PREREQUISITE for both integrity and privacy — you can't have accurate, appropriately-controlled data if it isn't reliably available and unchanged in the first place.
  • BUT security alone CANNOT guarantee either integrity or privacy — data can be perfectly secure (available, unchanged) while still being inaccurate, or while still being accessible to the wrong people.
Practice — Test Yourself

These questions only cover what THIS subsection taught — the definitions of integrity, privacy and security (completes Section 8.01).

Short answer 1
Give the precise definitions of data integrity, data privacy, and data security.
Reveal answer

Integrity: data is accurate and up to date. Privacy: data is available only to authorised users. Security: data is recoverable if lost or corrupted.

Short answer 2
Why do data protection laws matter for data privacy, given that they can't force perfect compliance?
Reveal answer

They act as a DETERRENT — wrongdoers who breach privacy can be subject to legal proceedings, even though the existence of a law can't by itself guarantee everyone follows it.

Fill in the blank 3
Data security is a ______ for data integrity and privacy — but by itself cannot ______ either.
Reveal answer

Prerequisite; guarantee.

Spot the error 4
A student says: "If data is secure (available, uncorrupted), it must also be accurate and appropriately private, since security covers everything." What's wrong?
Reveal answer

Security alone cannot guarantee integrity or privacy. Data can be perfectly secure (available exactly as originally stored) while still being factually WRONG (failing integrity), or while still being visible to unauthorised people (failing privacy).

Applied / scenario 5
A company's customer database is perfectly backed up and always available (secure), but a data-entry error means half the customer addresses are wrong. Which of the three properties (integrity/privacy/security) has been violated, and why?
Reveal answer

Integrity — the data is available and unchanged from what was stored (security is fine), but it isn't ACCURATE, which is exactly what integrity requires.

Short answer 6
What do most data protection laws mainly relate to, in terms of WHOSE data and WHY it was supplied?
Reveal answer

Personal (private) data that an individual supplies to an organisation, for a purpose understood and agreed by that individual — the law obliges the organisation to protect its privacy and integrity.
