16.03 Precision and Normalisation
Getting the most precision out of a fixed number of mantissa bits.
Theory

The mantissa/exponent trade-off

  • More bits for the MANTISSA → better PRECISION, but fewer bits left for the exponent → smaller RANGE.
  • In practice, the total number of bits is often choosable when writing a program, but the SPLIT between mantissa and exponent bits is usually fixed by the floating-point processor itself.

Why normalise?

  • NORMALISATION here is completely UNRELATED to database normalisation (Chapter 10) — same word, different concept entirely.
  • Since precision improves with more mantissa bits actually being USED meaningfully, optimum precision requires using the LARGEST possible magnitude for the mantissa's value.
  • The SAME denary value can be represented multiple different ways in floating-point (different mantissa/exponent combinations) — normalisation picks the ONE representation that maximises precision.

Worked example — three representations of denary 2

Denary representationFloating-point binary
0.125 × 2⁴0 0010 100
0.25 × 2³0 0100 011
0.5 × 2²0 1000 010
  • All three represent the SAME value (2), but 0.5 × 2² uses the mantissa's bits most fully — this is the NORMALISED form.
  • Recognising normalised form: for a POSITIVE number, the mantissa's two most significant bits are DIFFERENT (0 followed by 1).
  • For a NEGATIVE number, normalised form has the two most significant bits as 1 followed by 0 — for example, −4 normalised is −1.0 × 2², as opposed to less-precise alternatives like −0.25 × 2⁴.

How to normalise a value

  • For a POSITIVE number: shift the mantissa bits LEFT until the most significant bits read 0 followed by 1. Each left shift REDUCES the exponent by 1 (to keep the overall value the same).
  • For a NEGATIVE number: shift left until the most significant bits read 1 followed by 0 — bits falling off the most significant end during this process are simply discarded, with no special handling.
Practice — Test Yourself

These questions only cover what THIS subsection taught — precision and normalisation (part of Section 16.03).

Short answer 1
Why does increasing the number of mantissa bits improve precision but reduce range?
Reveal answer

More mantissa bits let a value be stored more exactly (better precision), but since the total number of bits is fixed, this leaves FEWER bits available for the exponent, which shrinks the range of magnitudes that can be represented.

Short answer 2
How can you recognise, just by looking at the bits, whether a POSITIVE floating-point number is normalised?
Reveal answer

Its mantissa's two most significant bits are different — specifically 0 followed by 1.

Fill in the blank 3
Normalisation in floating-point representation is completely ______ to normalisation in database design — the same word describes two ______ concepts.
Reveal answer

Unrelated; different.

Spot the error 4
When normalising a positive value, a student shifts the mantissa left by 3 places but forgets to change the exponent. What's wrong?
Reveal answer

Each left shift of the mantissa must be matched by REDUCING the exponent by 1 for each shift (3 shifts → exponent reduced by 3) — otherwise the represented value changes, since the mantissa's magnitude increased without the exponent compensating.

Applied / scenario 5
Of the three representations of denary 2 shown above (0.125×2⁴, 0.25×2³, 0.5×2²), which is normalised, and why?
Reveal answer

0.5 × 2² — it uses the mantissa's available bits most fully (the largest possible magnitude for the mantissa), which is what defines a normalised representation.

Short answer 6
What happens to bits that "fall off" the most significant end of the mantissa when normalising a negative number?
Reveal answer

They are simply discarded — no special handling or carrying is applied to them.
