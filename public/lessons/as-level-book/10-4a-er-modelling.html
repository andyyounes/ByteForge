10.04 Entity-Relationship Modelling
Four steps: choose entities, identify relationships, decide cardinalities, draw the full diagram.
Key Terms
Entity (entity type)
A thing, person type, event, transaction, or organisation — with multiple real instances. Becomes a table.
Cardinality
How many instances of one entity relate to how many instances of another (1:1, 1:M, M:1, M:M).
Theory

The requirement statement

  • "The agency needs a database to handle bookings for bands. Each band has a number of members. Each booking is for a venue. Each booking might be for one or more bands."

Step 1 — choose the entities (look for the nouns)

  • Ignore "agency" (only one exists). Choose: Booking, Band, Member, Venue — each has MULTIPLE real instances.
  • A useful simplification: even though a booking is really for a gig at a venue, the Booking entity alone is judged sufficient to hold the needed gig data.

Step 2 — identify the relationships (don't define too many)

  • Chosen: Booking↔Venue, Booking↔Band, Band↔Member.
  • A relationship between Member and Venue is deliberately IGNORED — it's assumed to be handled indirectly through the other relationships.

Step 3 — decide the cardinality of each relationship

  • Four possible cardinalities: one-to-one (1:1), one-to-many (1:M), many-to-one (M:1), many-to-many (M:M).
  • Each relationship line actually represents TWO relationships at once — a "forward" and a "backward" reading.

Working through each cardinality with reasoning

  • Member : Band — one Member is a member of only ONE Band (must be confirmed as a real fact by the agency); one Band has MORE THAN ONE Member. So the relationship is M:1.
  • Adding detail: a Member MUST belong to a Band, and a Band MUST have more than one Member — each end of the relationship line gets TWO symbols, one for MINIMUM cardinality, one for MAXIMUM. Here they happen to be the same value at each end.
  • Booking : Venue — one Booking is for exactly ONE Venue (there must be one, and only one); one Venue can be used for MANY Bookings — so M:1. However, a Venue might exist that has NEVER had a booking, so the minimum at the Venue end allows zero.
  • Band : Booking — one Booking can be for MANY Bands, and one Band has MANY Bookings (hopefully) — so M:M. But a new Band might not yet have any booking, and there might be only one Band for a given booking — so minimums allow zero/one at each end.

Step 4 — create the full, named ER diagram

  • Each relationship gets a NAME (e.g. "belongs to" / "has", "is for" / "is booked for", "booked for" / "made at").
  • Reading a relationship ALWAYS starts from one entity — even across a many-to-many relationship, you read forward, then backward, as two separate sentences.
Reading the Band↔Booking relationship both ways

"One Band is booked for zero or many Bookings."

"One Booking is for one or many Bands."

Practice — Test Yourself

These questions only cover what THIS subsection taught — the four-step ER modelling process (completes Section 10.04).

Short answer 1
List the four steps of the ER modelling process, in order.
Reveal answer

1. Choose the entities. 2. Identify the relationships. 3. Decide the cardinalities. 4. Create the full, named ER diagram.

Short answer 2
What is the cardinality of the Member:Band relationship, and why?
Reveal answer

M:1 — a Member belongs to only ONE Band (the "1" side), while a Band has MANY Members (the "M" side).

Fill in the blank 3
Each end of a relationship line carries TWO cardinality symbols: one for the ______ and one for the ______.
Reveal answer

Minimum; maximum.

Spot the error 4
A student says: "'Booking' entity is enough — we don't need a separate 'Venue' entity, since a booking already implies a venue happened." Why does the worked example keep them separate?
Reveal answer

A Venue exists independently and can be reused across MANY different Bookings (M:1 from Booking to Venue) — collapsing them would lose the ability to represent one venue hosting many bookings over time, and would duplicate venue details in every booking record.

Applied / scenario 5
Why does the Booking:Venue relationship allow a MINIMUM of zero at the Venue end, even though the cardinality is M:1?
Reveal answer

Because a Venue might exist in the database that has never actually had a booking made at it yet — so the minimum number of bookings for a given venue can genuinely be zero.

Short answer 6
Why was a possible Member↔Venue relationship deliberately left out of the model?
Reveal answer

It's assumed to be handled INDIRECTLY through the other relationships already chosen (Member→Band→Booking→Venue) — adding it directly would be redundant.
