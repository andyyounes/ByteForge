11.01 What Is an Algorithm?
A wheel change, a cake recipe, and a London Underground journey — algorithms are everywhere before code ever enters the picture.
Key Terms
Algorithm
A sequence of steps that can be carried out to perform a task.
Theory

Order matters — a wheel-change algorithm

  • Loosen wheel nuts → position jack → raise car → remove wheel/nuts → fit new wheel → replace nuts, hand-tighten → lower car → fully tighten nuts.
  • If the steps aren't followed in the correct LOGICAL sequence, the process becomes harder or IMPOSSIBLE — e.g. loosening nuts after raising the car means the wheel just spins.

A recipe is an algorithm — with real input/process/output

  • The ingredients are the INPUT; the finished cake is the OUTPUT; mixing and baking is the PROCESS.
  • A step can be broken down further into smaller sub-steps (e.g. "mix ingredients" → beat sugar/butter, add eggs one at a time, sieve and stir in flour, add milk) — algorithms can be described at different levels of detail.

Different steps depending on a condition — the London Underground example

  • Getting from King's Cross to Westminster: Route A (Victoria line, 4 stops, then Jubilee) is normally best — UNLESS there are engineering works on the Victoria line, in which case Route B (Piccadilly, 6 stops, then Jubilee) is better.
IF there are engineering works on the Victoria Line THEN Take the Piccadilly Line to Green Park (6 stations) Take the Jubilee Line to Westminster (1 station) ELSE Take the Victoria Line to Green Park (4 stations) Take the Jubilee Line to Westminster (1 station)

Many problems have more than one valid solution

  • Sometimes choosing between solutions is purely personal preference; sometimes one solution is genuinely BETTER than another (faster, uses less memory, easier to understand).
Practice — Test Yourself

These questions only cover what THIS subsection taught — what an algorithm is (completes Section 11.01).

Short answer 1
Give the precise definition of an algorithm.
Reveal answer

A sequence of steps that can be carried out to perform a task.

Short answer 2
In the cake recipe, what is the input and what is the output?
Reveal answer

Input: the ingredients. Output: the finished cake.

Fill in the blank 3
If a wheel-change algorithm's steps aren't followed in the correct ______ sequence, the process can become much harder or even impossible.
Reveal answer

Logical.

Spot the error 4
A student says: "A good algorithm should always be written at the most detailed level possible, breaking every single step down as far as it will go." Does this lesson support that?
Reveal answer

Not necessarily — the recipe example shows a step CAN be broken down further when useful (e.g. "mix ingredients" into 4 sub-steps), but algorithms are described at whatever level of detail is appropriate for the task, not always the maximum possible.

Applied / scenario 5
In the Underground example, what real-world CONDITION determines which of the two routes should be followed?
Reveal answer

Whether there are engineering works on the Victoria Line — if so, take the Piccadilly Line route instead of the normally-faster Victoria Line route.

Short answer 6
Is it always the case that one solution to a problem is objectively "better" than another? What does this lesson say?
Reveal answer

No — sometimes it's purely personal preference which solution to choose; sometimes one genuinely is better than another (e.g. faster, less memory).
