1.06 Video Representation
A video is more than "a bunch of still images" — refresh rate and encoding both matter to the eye.
Key Terms
Frame rate
The number of frames (still images) displayed per second.
Interlaced encoding
Each frame split into odd and even lines, displayed as two halves — halves the bandwidth needed.
Progressive encoding
A full frame is displayed all at once, every time.
Theory

Why it's not "just a stack of photos"

  • A frame is created and displayed line by line — resolution is defined by lines per frame AND pixels per line, and must be compatible between the stored video and the actual display screen.
  • The human eye has real physical limits that constrain the design: below a 50 Hz refresh rate, the eye notices FLICKER. But as long as the refresh rate is at least 25 Hz, the eye can't tell that the MOTION itself isn't perfectly continuous.
  • This creates a genuine tension: 50 Hz avoids flicker, but sending a full new frame 50 times a second needs a lot of bandwidth.
Interlaced encodingEach frame is split into two halves — odd-numbered lines, then even-numbered lines — displayed one after the other. This achieves what LOOKS like a high refresh rate to the eye, while actually halving the real transmission bandwidth needed. Used historically in TV broadcasting, then adapted for video recording.
Progressive encodingA FULL frame is displayed every single time — no splitting into halves. Needs more bandwidth than interlacing, but avoids interlacing's visual artefacts. As transmission bandwidth improves, this is becoming the norm.

The genuine trade-off

  • Interlacing was a clever historical solution to a real bandwidth CONSTRAINT — it doesn't send more data, it sends HALF the data per moment while still satisfying the eye's flicker threshold.
  • As bandwidth has become less of a constraint, progressive encoding (simpler, and free of interlacing's specific visual artefacts) has become increasingly practical and preferred.
Practice — Test Yourself

These questions only cover what THIS subsection taught — video refresh rates and interlaced vs. progressive encoding.

Short answer 1
Below what refresh rate does the eye notice flicker?
Reveal answer

Below 50 times per second (50 Hz).

Short answer 2
What refresh rate is enough for the eye to perceive motion as continuous, even if it's technically not?
Reveal answer

25 times per second (25 Hz).

Fill in the blank 3
Interlaced encoding splits each frame into ______-numbered lines and ______-numbered lines, displayed separately.
Reveal answer

Odd-numbered lines and even-numbered lines.

Spot the error 4
A student says interlacing sends MORE data per second than progressive encoding, since it involves two separate halves per frame. Why is this backwards?
Reveal answer

Interlacing sends LESS data — each half-frame contains only half the lines of a full frame, so transmitting two halves (odd then even) uses roughly HALF the bandwidth of transmitting a genuinely full frame each time (progressive encoding), while still appearing to refresh at a similar rate to the eye.

Applied / scenario 5
Explain why interlaced encoding became LESS necessary as internet/broadcast bandwidth improved over time.
Reveal answer

Interlacing's whole purpose was to reduce the bandwidth needed to satisfy the eye's flicker threshold — once bandwidth became less of a genuine constraint, there was less need for that trade-off, and progressive encoding (a full frame every time) became increasingly practical.

Short answer 6
What TWO things define a video's resolution?
Reveal answer

The number of lines per frame, and the number of pixels per line.
