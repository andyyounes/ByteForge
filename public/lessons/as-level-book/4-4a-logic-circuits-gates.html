4.04 Logic Circuits and Logic Gates
Building a real circuit from a problem statement, then tracing it into a truth table systematically.
Key Terms
Logic gate
A component of a logic circuit whose operation matches that of a Boolean operator.
Theory

Digital circuits are built from transistors, viewed as logic gates

  • Every part of a digital circuit is, at any moment, either "on" (1) or "off" (0).
  • Physically, this is built from integrated circuits made of transistors — potentially BILLIONS of them in one chip.
  • At the level this course works at, we view a circuit as made of LOGIC GATES — each one matching a specific Boolean operator.
  • Symbol convention: the GATE'S SHAPE defines its type; inputs are on the LEFT, output on the RIGHT. This course only considers gates with at most two inputs (though real circuits can have more).
  • The NOT gate is a special case with only ONE input. NAND and NOR are each an AND/OR gate combined with a NOT gate — producing the complementary output to AND/OR respectively.
Worked Example — building a circuit from a problem statement

A bank offers a special lending rate if: the customer has held an account for 2 years (A), AND at least TWO of the following also apply: married (B), aged 25+ (C), parents are customers (D).

Logic expression: Special_rate IF A AND (((B AND C) OR (B AND D)) OR (C AND D))

Note the EXTRA brackets — not strictly required by operator precedence, but included deliberately to guide building the circuit where each gate only accepts two inputs.

This expression needs FOUR AND gates and TWO OR gates to build.

The general method — from problem statement to circuit

  • Step 1: convert the problem statement into a formal logic expression.
  • Step 2: identify each individual Boolean operation inside that expression.
  • Step 3: represent each condition with a single letter/symbol (A, B, C, …) — just like in ordinary algebra.
  • Step 4: build the circuit gate by gate, using brackets in the expression as a guide for how gates should be grouped and connected (especially important when gates are limited to two inputs each).
Worked Example — tracing a 3-input circuit into a truth table

With 3 inputs (A, B, C), the truth table needs 2³ = 8 rows, listed in increasing binary order: 000, 001, 010, 011, 100, 101, 110, 111.

Key method: don't try to work out the final output directly from the inputs. Instead, identify INTERMEDIATE points in the circuit (e.g. label them M, N, P, Q — one after each gate) and fill in a "workspace" column for each, working through the circuit systematically.

Reading the finished table, and a genuinely useful sanity check

  • This particular circuit has exactly TWO input combinations that produce a TRUE (1) output.
  • Once you're confident in the workspace columns, they can be DELETED — only the inputs and final output need to remain in the presented answer.
  • Useful check: trace just PART of the circuit. If, say, the path from input C to the output passes through two AND gates, then EVERY row where C = 0 must give output 0 — letting you sanity-check roughly half the table at a glance before checking the rest in detail.

Short answer 1
Why does this course only consider gates with at most TWO inputs, even though real logic gates can have more?
Reveal answer

It keeps circuit construction and analysis manageable and consistent for the course — real integrated circuits genuinely can and do use gates with more inputs.

Short answer 2
Why does the worked bank-rate example include "extra" brackets that aren't strictly needed by normal operator precedence?
Reveal answer

To guide the construction of the circuit itself — since each gate can only accept two inputs, the brackets show exactly how to group conditions pairwise into individual gates.

Spot the error 4
A student lists the rows of an 8-row truth table in this order: 000, 001, 011, 010, 100... What's wrong, and why does it matter?
Reveal answer

Rows must be listed in INCREASING binary order (000, 001, 010, 011, 100...) — the student swapped 011 and 010. This matters because it's easy to accidentally omit or duplicate a combination if you don't follow a systematic order.

Applied / scenario 5
In the worked 3-input example, the path from input C to the final output X passes through two AND gates in series. Without recalculating every row, what can you immediately conclude about every row where C = 0?
Reveal answer

Every row where C = 0 must produce output X = 0 — since C feeds into a chain of AND gates on its way to the output, and AND requires ALL its inputs to be TRUE.

Short answer 6
Why are NAND and NOR gates described as combinations of another gate plus a NOT gate?
Reveal answer

NAND produces exactly the complementary (opposite) output to AND for every input combination; NOR produces exactly the complementary output to OR — as if a NOT gate had been placed straight after an AND or OR gate.
