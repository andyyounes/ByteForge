10.2e Real-World Problems into Logic Circuits
Safety systems, alarms, sensors — turning a written scenario into a genuine logic expression.
Theory

The strategy — hunt for AND, OR, NOT hiding in the words

  • ON = 1, OFF = 0 — and remember 0 is usually treated as "NOT 1".
  • Look for the key words connecting conditions: "and" → AND, "or" → OR, "not"/negative phrasing → NOT.
  • Build the expression piece by piece, one condition at a time, before combining them.
Worked example — a safety alarm

"An alarm, X, sounds if input A represents ON and input B represents OFF; OR if input B represents ON and input C represents OFF."

Turning the words into an expression

  • "A is ON and B is OFF" → (A AND NOT B).
  • "B is ON and C is OFF" → (B AND NOT C).
  • These two parts are joined by "OR" in the original sentence: (A AND NOT B) OR (B AND NOT C).
  • The circuit is built the same way as any expression (10.2c): build each half separately, then join with the OR gate mentioned.

A harder real-world example — a wind turbine safety system

Three sensors: turbine speed (S: 1 if >1000 rpm), bearing temperature (T: 1 if >80°C), wind velocity (W: 1 if >120 kph). Shutdown (X=1) happens if: (speed ≤1000 AND temp >80°C) OR (speed >1000 AND wind >120kph) OR (temp ≤80°C AND wind >120kph).

Converting a table of conditions, one clause at a time

  • "speed ≤1000 rpm and bearing temp >80°C" → (NOT S AND T).
  • "speed >1000 rpm and wind >120kph" → (S AND W).
  • "bearing temp ≤80°C and wind >120kph" → (NOT T AND W).
  • All three original clauses were joined by "OR" — so the three intermediate circuits are joined using TWO OR gates (since OR only takes 2 inputs at a time): first join (i) and (ii), then join that result with (iii).

A genuinely useful check for complex, real problems

  • For a problem this complex, it's advisable to produce the logic circuit AND the truth table SEPARATELY (each straight from the original conditions), then compare them against each other.
  • Any disagreement between the two reveals a mistake — either in the circuit's wiring, or in how a condition was translated into logic.
Practice — Test Yourself

These questions only cover what THIS subsection taught — converting a real-world problem statement into a logic expression.

Short answer 1
In a problem statement, what English word usually signals an AND, and what usually signals an OR?
Reveal answer

"and" signals AND; "or" signals OR. Negative phrasing (e.g. "is OFF", "is NOT") signals NOT.

Short answer 2
In the safety alarm example, what expression represents "input A represents ON and input B represents OFF"?
Reveal answer

(A AND NOT B).

Fill in the blank pseudocode 3
Complete the wind turbine clause "bearing temp ≤80°C and wind >120kph": (______ T AND W)
Reveal answer

(NOT T AND W).

Spot the error 4
A student translates "bearing temperature > 80°C" directly as just T (forgetting this IS what T=1 already means), then separately treats "temperature ≤ 80°C" as also just T. What's the mistake?
Reveal answer

"Temperature > 80°C" corresponds to T=1 (written simply as T), but "temperature ≤ 80°C" is the OPPOSITE condition and must be written as NOT T — treating both phrasings as plain T would make two genuinely different conditions indistinguishable.

Applied / scenario 5
Using the safety alarm's truth table, what is X when A=1, B=1, C=0?
Reveal answer

X = 1 (row 7 in the table) — B is ON and C is OFF, satisfying the second half of the alarm condition.

Short answer 6
Why is it good practice to build the circuit AND the truth table separately for a complex real-world problem, then compare them?
Reveal answer

Any disagreement between the two independently-produced results reveals a genuine mistake somewhere — either in translating a condition into logic, or in how the circuit was wired — a check that's especially valuable the more complex the problem gets.
