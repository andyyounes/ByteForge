2.3a Encryption & Symmetric Encryption
Why encryption exists, plaintext vs. ciphertext, and the single-key encryption approach — plus its real weakness.
Key Terms
Eavesdropper
A hacker who intercepts data being transmitted over a public network.
Plaintext
The original, readable data before encryption.
Ciphertext
Plaintext after it has been transformed by an encryption algorithm — unreadable without the right key.
Symmetric encryption
Encryption where the SAME key is used to both encrypt and decrypt the message.
Theory

Why encryption is needed

  • Data transmitted over ANY public network (wired or wireless) always risks interception by a hacker — called an eavesdropper in this context.
  • Encryption does NOT prevent the data being intercepted — it stops the intercepted data from making any SENSE to the eavesdropper.
  • This matters especially for sensitive/confidential data: credit card or bank details, medical history, legal documents.
  • Original data is called plaintext. After going through an encryption algorithm (using an encryption key), it becomes ciphertext. A matching decryption algorithm and key turn it back into plaintext.
Plaintext → encryption algorithm + key → ciphertext (and back again with the decryption key).

How symmetric encryption works

  • Symmetric encryption uses ONE encryption key, and the SAME key is used to decrypt the message.
  • Simple example: a 10-digit denary key shifts each letter across the alphabet by a different amount per position.
Worked example — Caesar-style shift cipher

Encryption key: 4 2 9 1 3 (repeating). Message: "COMPU" (first 5 letters of "COMPUTER").

C+4G
O+2Q
M+9V
P+1Q
U+3X

To decrypt, the SAME key is applied in reverse — each ciphertext letter is shifted BACKWARDS by the same amount (e.g. G → −4 → C, Q → −2 → O, V → −9 → M).

The real weakness of symmetric encryption

  • A simple 10-digit denary key gives only 10¹⁰ possible codes — modern computers can crack this in seconds.
  • Real systems now use 256-bit binary keys, giving about 2²⁵⁶ ≈ 1.2 × 10⁷⁷ possible combinations — vastly harder to crack (though quantum computing may eventually threaten even this).
  • The GENUINE difficulty isn't the key's strength — it's keeping the key SECRET. The same key must somehow reach both sender AND receiver (e.g. via email or text), and that transfer itself can be intercepted.
  • This is symmetric encryption's main drawback: security depends entirely on a single shared key that both parties must have — and safely exchanging that key is a genuine, unsolved problem on its own.
Practice — Test Yourself

These questions only cover what THIS subsection taught — the purpose of encryption and symmetric encryption.

Short answer 1
Does encryption prevent data from being intercepted? Explain what it actually does instead.
Reveal answer

No — encryption does NOT stop data being intercepted. It stops the intercepted data from making any SENSE to the eavesdropper, since it's transformed into unreadable ciphertext without the correct key.

Short answer 2
In symmetric encryption, how many distinct keys are needed to both encrypt AND decrypt a message?
Reveal answer

Just one — the same key is used for both encryption and decryption.

Fill in the blank 3
Original readable data is called ______; after encryption it becomes ______.
Reveal answer

Blank 1: plaintext. Blank 2: ciphertext.

Spot the error 4
A student claims: "Symmetric encryption is completely secure as long as you use a long enough key, like 256-bit." What real vulnerability does this ignore?
Reveal answer

Key LENGTH doesn't solve the real problem — the difficulty is safely getting the SAME secret key to both sender and receiver in the first place. However long the key, if it's intercepted during that exchange (e.g. sent via email or text), the encryption is compromised regardless of key strength.

Applied / scenario 5
Two colleagues need to exchange confidential financial data regularly and want to use symmetric encryption. Identify the single biggest practical challenge they'll face in setting this up, and explain why, using this lesson's content.
Reveal answer

The biggest challenge is securely SHARING the single symmetric key between them in the first place — the key itself needs to be transmitted somehow (e.g. email, text), and that transmission is exactly the kind of public-network transfer that risks interception. This is the exact drawback the lesson identifies as symmetric encryption's main weakness.

Short answer 6
Give one real example of the kind of sensitive data that encryption is especially important for protecting.
Reveal answer

Any of: credit card / bank details, medical history, legal documents.
