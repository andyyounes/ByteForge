        \item A Real Message
        \item Strangers Never Need Your Address
        \item A Friend, Asking Normally
        \item[$\bigstar$] BOSS --- A New One
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A surprise free prize is the \#1 real scam sign}
    \large
    \begin{itemize}
        \item "You've won 10,000 free V-Bucks
        \item Click here!"
        \item Nobody gives away expensive prizes to random strangers who never entered anything; the free prize IS the bait, not a real gift.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Not every message is a scam}
    \large
    \begin{itemize}
        \item "Your teacher has posted a new assignment, due Friday" has none of the real red flags
        \item No prize, no urgency, no request for personal info
        \item Genuinely just a normal message.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{A stranger from an online game has no real reason to need your address}
    \large
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Being friendly in chat does not mean someone actually is who they say they are
        \item Asking for a real name, school, or home address is a genuine red flag, even from someone who seems nice.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{A message from someone you actually know, asking normally, is not a scam}
    \large
    \begin{itemize}
        \item "want to team up for the next match?" from a real friend has no red flags at all
        \item Judging every message the same way means also correctly recognizing the safe ones.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Real-World Examples}
    \large
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Phishing messages promising free prizes or gift cards are a genuine, extremely common real attack
        \item Not a hypothetical classroom example
        \item Costing real victims real money and account access every single day.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
